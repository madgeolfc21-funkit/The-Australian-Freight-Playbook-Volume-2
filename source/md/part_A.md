## Chapter 1: How Australia Moves Freight

Road carries most of the freight you will ever book; rail carries most of the weight. In 2024 to 25, rail moved about 447 billion tonne kilometres and road about 253 billion ([BITRE Yearbook 2026](https://www.bitre.gov.au/freight-chapter-yearbook-2026)). Most of rail's share is iron ore, coal and grain: over 70% of bulk freight moved by rail, but only 16% of non bulk freight ([Infrastructure Australia](https://www.infrastructureaustralia.gov.au/ipl/high-productivity-freight-networks/rail-freight)).

For general freight (cartons, pallets, machinery), the answer depends on the corridor:

- **The east coast is a road market.** Rail carries only about 2% of Melbourne to Sydney freight. Inland Rail was paused at Parkes in May 2026, with Beveridge to Parkes due by 2027.
- **East to west is a rail market.** Rail's share of non bulk freight is highest on the corridor to Perth.
- **Tasmania is a sea market.** Every interstate move crosses Bass Strait. The Spirit of Tasmania now sails from Geelong to Devonport; Spirit of Tasmania IV made its first sailing on 31 October 2026.
- **The north is road and rail.** Adelaide to Darwin has both; Queensland to Darwin is road.

### Typical Transit Times

| Lane | Typical road transit, general freight | Other options | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sydney to Canberra | Same day or next day | None in practice | Cut off times for same day |
| Sydney to Melbourne | 1 to 2 business days | Rail, about 2% share | Hume Highway: heavy B double traffic, overnight linehaul |
| Sydney to Brisbane | 1 to 2 business days | Rail and coastal shipping, little general freight | Pacific Highway or New England Highway route |
| Melbourne to Adelaide | 1 to 2 business days | Rail | Western Highway closures in fire season |
| Sydney to Adelaide | 2 to 3 business days | Rail via Broken Hill | Long single carriageway sections |
| Melbourne to Brisbane | 2 to 3 business days | Rail via Sydney; Inland Rail not yet open | Newell Highway |
| Eastern capitals to Perth | 4 to 6 business days | Rail leads this corridor | Nullarbor: fuel range, heat, animals |
| Adelaide to Darwin | 3 to 4 business days | Rail via Alice Springs | Wet season in the north |
| Melbourne to Tasmania | 2 to 3 business days | Sea only | Sailing cut off times and the sea leg |

Transit times are typical for general road freight in business days, door to door, and vary by carrier and service. Your own carrier's lane times go in T06.

## Chapter 2: The Vocabulary

Freight has its own language, and the paperwork assumes you speak it. These are the words used throughout Volume 2, grouped by where you meet them.

### On the Booking and the Invoice

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Parcel | Small items a courier network handles by hand, priced per item |
| Pallet freight | Freight on a pallet, moved by forklift, priced per pallet or per kg |
| LTL (less than truckload) | Your freight shares a truck with other customers' freight; priced by weight or space |
| FTL (full truckload) | You book the whole truck; priced per truck or per km, door to door |
| Cubic weight | Size turned into a weight: length × width × height in metres × a cubic factor (often 250 kg per cubic metre for road) |
| Chargeable weight | The greater of the actual weight and the cubic weight; what you pay on |
| Rate card | The carrier's price list by zone, weight break and service |
| Fuel levy | A percentage added to freight to cover diesel price movement (Part B, Chapter 4) |
| Surcharge | A fee for extra work: tail lift, residential, waiting time, remote area |

### On the Paperwork

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Con note (consignment note) | The document for one consignment: from, to, items, weight, instructions. Signed at pickup |
| Manifest | The list of all con notes on one pickup or one truck |
| POD (proof of delivery) | The receiver's signature, name, date and time, sometimes a photo |
| DG (dangerous goods) | Goods classed as hazardous for transport under the Australian Dangerous Goods Code; need declaring |
| Work diary | The driver's legal record of work and rest hours (Part C) |

### In the Depot and on the Road

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Pickup and delivery (P and D) | Local trucks collecting from senders and delivering to receivers around a city |
| Linehaul | The long run between depots, usually overnight, usually a semi or B double |
| Cross dock | Freight unloaded from one truck and loaded straight onto another without being stored |
| Backload | A load for the return trip, so the truck does not come home empty |
| Tail lift | A powered platform on the back of a truck for delivering where there is no forklift |
| 3PL (third party logistics) | A business that stores, picks, packs and ships for other businesses |

### On the Truck

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| GVM (gross vehicle mass) | The most a vehicle may weigh fully loaded, as set by the manufacturer |
| GCM (gross combination mass) | The most a truck and everything it tows may weigh together, as set by the manufacturer |
| ATM (aggregate trailer mass) | The most a trailer may weigh fully loaded |
| Tare | The weight of the empty vehicle |
| Payload | What you can actually carry: GVM (or GCM) less tare, and less any lower legal limit |
| Axle group | Axles that share the load: single, tandem (two), tri (three). Each group has its own legal limit |
| PBS (Performance Based Standards) | Vehicles approved on how they perform rather than fixed size rules; often bigger and more productive |

### In the Law

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| HVNL | Heavy Vehicle National Law, which covers every state and territory except WA and the NT |
| NHVR | National Heavy Vehicle Regulator, which runs the HVNL |
| CoR (Chain of Responsibility) | Everyone who influences a heavy vehicle trip shares a duty to keep it safe (Chapter 3) |
| NHVAS and HVA | Heavy vehicle accreditation schemes; NHVAS is moving to Heavy Vehicle Accreditation (Part D) |

## Chapter 3: Who Does What

A single pallet can pass through six businesses between the dock and the receiver. Each has a name on the paperwork, and under the Heavy Vehicle National Law each can be a party in the Chain of Responsibility. The law looks at what a business actually does, so one company can hold several roles at once.

&#91;embedded content: Parties in a road consignment and the Chain of Responsibility roles they usually hold\]

The arrow that matters most is the dashed one. Many carriers hand freight to a subcontractor, and the subcontractor's driver is the one on the road. The duties of everyone above them do not disappear when that happens.

| Name on the paperwork | What they do | Chain of Responsibility role when they do it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shipper | Owns the need for the freight and pays for it | Whatever it actually does: usually consignor, sometimes packer and loader too |
| Consignor | Hands the goods to the carrier | Consignor |
| Consignee (receiver) | Takes delivery; often sets the delivery window | Consignee, and unloader if its staff unload |
| Packer | Puts goods into packages, onto pallets or into containers | Packer |
| Loader and loading manager | Loads the truck, or runs the site where loading happens | Loader, loading manager |
| Carrier | Runs the truck business that moves the freight | Operator, employer, scheduler, prime contractor |
| Subcontract carrier | Moves freight for another carrier | Operator, and driver if one person does both |
| Freight forwarder or broker | Arranges transport on the shipper's behalf | Often scheduler or consignor, by what it controls |
| 3PL | Stores, picks, packs and books freight for others | Often packer, loader, loading manager and consignor |
| Driver | Drives the vehicle | Has direct duties of their own as the driver (Part C) |

The parties the law names are the employer, prime contractor, operator, scheduler, consignor, consignee, packer, loading manager, loader and unloader. Executive officers of each business carry their own due diligence duty. Part B Chapter 7 covers the shipper's side and Part D the operator's.

## Chapter 4: The Vehicle Ladder

Each step up the ladder carries more freight for less cost per pallet, and asks more of the driver's licence and the road. The semi trailer is the workhorse of Australian linehaul: 22 pallets, one driver, general access on most roads.

&#91;embedded content: Typical pallet counts; actual numbers depend on body length, axle layout and pallet weight. Licence classes are national.\]

| Vehicle | Licence | Mass guide | Where it can go |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Van or ute | C | Up to 4.5 t GVM | Anywhere a car can go |
| Light rigid | LR | Over 4.5 t, up to 8 t GVM | General access |
| Medium rigid (2 axles) | MR | Over 8 t GVM | General access. Over 12 t GVM, the fatigue rules apply to the driver |
| Heavy rigid (3 or more axles) | HR | Tandem drive group 17 t from 1 August 2026 | General access |
| Semi trailer (6 axles) | HC | 43.5 t general mass limit from 1 August 2026 | General access; general access length rose from 19 m to 20 m on 1 August 2026 |
| B double | MC | Tri axle groups 21 t each | B double network only, under a notice or permit |
| B triple | MC | Above B double | Approved networks only; Victoria by permit only |
| Road train (Type 1 and Type 2) | MC | Set by state notice | Road train networks in the west, north and inland; Victoria allows A doubles only |
| PBS combinations | Depends on the combination | Set by the PBS approval | The PBS network level it was approved for |

Two rules of thumb follow from the ladder:

- **Mass or space runs out first, never both.** Light, bulky freight fills the deck before it reaches the mass limit; steel and liquids hit the mass limit with half the deck empty. Part D shows how to check both (T11 and T12).
- **Bigger is cheaper per pallet only when it is full.** A half full B double costs more per pallet than a full semi. Match the vehicle to the load, not the other way round.

## Chapter 5: Licence Classes

Australia uses the same six licence classes everywhere, and each step up needs time on the class below. The classes are national; the tests, fees and training rules are set by each state.

| Class | What you can drive | Towing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| C | Cars, utes and vans up to 4.5 t GVM | Light trailers |
| LR (light rigid) | Trucks over 4.5 t, up to 8 t GVM | A trailer up to 9 t GVM |
| MR (medium rigid) | Trucks over 8 t GVM with 2 axles | A trailer up to 9 t GVM |
| HR (heavy rigid) | Trucks over 8 t GVM with 3 or more axles | A trailer up to 9 t GVM |
| HC (heavy combination) | Prime mover and semi trailer; rigid towing a trailer over 9 t GVM | As shown |
| MC (multi combination) | B doubles, B triples, road trains and every other heavy vehicle | As shown |

&#91;embedded content: Licence upgrade path, NSW tenure rules as published by the NSW Government, checked November 2026\]

### The National Framework Is Changing the Path

Austroads and the state licensing agencies are rolling out the National Heavy Vehicle Driver Competency Framework. It ends the jump from HR straight to MC that most states allow today, and gives three ways to qualify for each upgrade ([Austroads pathways fact sheet](https://austroads.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0035/616967/NHVDCF_Factsheet2_Pathways.pdf)):

| Upgrade | Time held | Or driving experience | Or supervision program |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| MR or HR to HC | 1 year | 600 hours over at least 6 months | 420 work hours over at least 3 months, with 12 hours supervised driving |
| HC to MC | 1 year on HC | 700 hours over at least 6 months | 560 work hours over at least 4 months, with 16 hours supervised driving |

Austroads expects the framework to be phased in over several years, and no state start dates were published at November 2026. Until your state changes its rules, the current rules apply.

### Before You Upgrade

- **Check your state's rules.** Tenure, test type and provisional rules differ. In NSW, provisional (P2) MR or HR time counts toward HC, but you must hold an unrestricted licence before you can learn to drive the next class ([NSW Government](https://www.nsw.gov.au/driving-boating-and-transport/driver-and-rider-licences/heavy-vehicle-licences/getting-a-heavy-vehicle-licence)).
- **A licence is not a job ready driver.** An HC licence lets you drive a semi; it does not teach load restraint, fatigue rules or work diaries. Part C covers those.
- **Employers check the licence, every time.** Part D shows how to record licence checks for each driver.

## Chapter 6: Australian Conditions

Australian freight is shaped by distance more than anything else. Long gaps between towns, fuel and phone coverage turn a breakdown or a road closure into a day lost, and they shape what a fair transit time and a fair rate look like.

| Condition | What it does to freight | How to plan for it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Distance | Long hauls between towns; few places to stop, refuel or repair | Plan fuel and rest stops before departure; carry spares and water |
| Heat | Tyre blowouts, engine and brake strain, driver fatigue, heat damaged goods | Refrigerated or insulated freight for anything heat sensitive; early starts |
| Wet season (roughly November to April in the north) | Floods close roads in the NT, northern WA and north Queensland, sometimes for weeks | Build buffer time; ask carriers for their wet season routes and stock up early |
| Bushfire and flood season in the south | Sudden closures of main highways | Check the state traffic website before dispatch; have a backup route or mode |
| Animals | Kangaroos at dawn and dusk; cattle and camels on unfenced outback roads | Avoid dawn and dusk driving where possible; bull bars and lights on remote trucks |
| Mobile black spots | No phone coverage for long stretches in remote areas | Satellite phone or personal locator beacon; set check in times |
| Remote recovery | A tow or a mechanic can be hundreds of kilometres away | Breakdown cover that includes remote recovery; know the nearest service points |
| Road trains | Very long, heavy combinations sharing two lane roads | Give room; plan overtaking on long straights; road trains are covered in Part D |

### The Human Cost

Every condition in that table is also a safety risk. Deaths in crashes involving heavy vehicles have held at around 200 a year, and the trend has not improved.

&#91;embedded content: Australian Road Safety Data Hub, quarterly heavy vehicle road deaths, calendar year totals 2021 to 2025. January to June 2026: 100 deaths. Checked November 2026.\]

Those numbers include crashes where the truck driver was not at fault. They are still the reason fatigue, speed, mass and load restraint rules exist, and the reason the Chain of Responsibility reaches every business that sends, loads, schedules or receives freight.
