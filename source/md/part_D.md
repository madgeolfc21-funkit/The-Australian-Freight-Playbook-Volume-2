## Chapter 1: What Changed on 1 August 2026

The amended Heavy Vehicle National Law took effect on 1 August 2026, and it changed mass, length, fatigue, accreditation and penalties on the same day. Most of it lets operators carry more with less paperwork; some of it raises the stakes when things go wrong. This page applies to the HVNL states and territories. WA has adopted the same mass figures in its orders and permits, but its own legislation change was still pending in November 2026; check Main Roads WA.

### Mass and Size

| Rule | Before 1 August 2026 | From 1 August 2026 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| General mass limits | Lower general limits; higher limits needed concessional accreditation | The old concessional limits became general: plus 1 t for combinations under 55 t, plus 2 t over 55 t, no permit needed |
| Tandem axle group | 16.5 t | 17 t |
| Tri axle group | 20 t | 21 t |
| 6 axle semi trailer | 42.5 t | 43.5 t |
| Euro VI trucks | No allowance | Up to 500 kg extra across steer and drive axles, with conditions; steer axle up to 7 t on 315 mm or wider tyres |
| General access length | 19 m | 20 m, subject to wheelbase and equipment rules. B double lengths unchanged for now |
| Tow mass ratio | 1 to 1 | 1 to 1.3 |

### Fatigue and Fitness

- **Alternative Compliance Hours (ACH)** replace Basic and Advanced Fatigue Management. Existing accreditations run until they expire.
- **Standard hours did not change.** The solo and two up tables are the same (Part C, Chapter 3).
- **EWD tolerance:** 8 minutes on maximum work time in any period of 24 hours or less. Written diaries still round to 15 minute blocks.
- **Simpler written diary sheets:** no day of week, no standard hours tick, no daily totals.
- **A new duty not to drive unfit:** fatigue, illness, injury, drugs, alcohol or mental fitness, for heavy vehicles over 4.5 t.

### Accreditation and Penalties

- **NHVAS becomes Heavy Vehicle Accreditation (HVA),** with General Safety Accreditation and Alternative Compliance Accreditation. Transition runs for up to three years (Chapter 8).
- **71 penalties changed:** 50 went up and 21 came down.
- **Asking, directing or contracting a driver** to speed, drive tired or drive unfit now carries up to $28,230 for an offence committed between 1 August 2026 and 30 June 2027 (NHVR penalty list, document 910).
- **Severe mass breaches** start at $14,120, plus $710 for each 1% over, up to $28,230.

### What to Do This Month

1. Update every mass figure in your load plans and quoting tools (T11 and T12 already use the new limits).
2. Check whether your Euro VI trucks qualify for the extra mass, and record how.
3. If you run BFM or AFM, note the expiry date and plan the move to ACH.
4. Brief schedulers and customers on the new unfit to drive duty: it applies to them as well as the driver.
5. Diarise the penalty update: amounts index each year on 1 July.

## Chapter 2: Mass and Dimensions

A truck is legal only when three numbers all pass: each axle group is under its limit, the whole vehicle is under its combination limit, and neither is over the manufacturer's GVM or GCM. The lowest of those three is your real limit.

### Weigh It, Do Not Guess It

Weigh each truck empty once, axle group by axle group, and keep the tickets. From then on, T11 adds each pallet's weight and position and shows the load on every group. A weighbridge ticket is still the only proof: use one whenever a load is near a limit.

## Chapter 3: Loading and Restraint

A load must stay put under hard braking, cornering and rough roads. The national Load Restraint Guide sets the standard: restraint must hold 0.8 of the load's weight forward, 0.5 sideways, 0.5 rearward, and 0.2 upward when friction is relied on. The 2018 guide is still current; the June 2025 edition only changed the look.

### Two Ways to Restrain a Load

- **Direct restraint:** the load is blocked or contained, by the headboard, gates, sides, or lashings tied directly to the load. Best for heavy items.
- **Tie down:** lashings over the top clamp the load onto the deck, and friction holds it. Best for pallets and general freight, but only as good as the friction.

For tie down, each lashing holds this much of the load's weight:

```latex
W_{\text{per lashing}} = \frac{2 \times T \times \sin\theta \times \mu}{g - \mu}
```

T is the lashing's pre tension in kgf, θ the angle from the deck, μ the friction between load and deck, and g the force to resist (0.8 forward, 0.5 sideways or rearward). The NHVR case study checks out: an 8,000 kg block on timber (friction 0.4), with two lashings at 75 degrees and 750 kgf, holds about 2,800 kg forward. On a rubber mat (0.6) the same two lashings hold about 8,600 kg.

&#91;embedded content: Lashings worked out with the Load Restraint Guide 2018 tie down method; friction values are examples (0.4 and 0.6 from the NHVR worked example)\]

Fix the friction before you add straps. A rubber mat is bought once and works on every load; extra straps have to be thrown and tensioned every time.

### Pre Tension by Lashing Type

| Lashing | Tensioner | Pre tension, kgf |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 25 mm webbing | Hand ratchet | 100 |
| 35 mm webbing | Hand ratchet | 250 |
| 50 mm webbing | Truck winch or push up ratchet | 300 |
| 50 mm webbing | Push down ratchet | 600 |
| 50 mm webbing | High pre tension ratchet | Up to 750 |
| Chain, 7 mm and above | Turnbuckle | 1,000 |

### Who Is Liable When the Load Shifts

Everyone who had a hand in it. The driver must not drive an unsafe load. The loader, loading manager, packer and consignor have their own duties under the Chain of Responsibility, and so does the operator who sent the truck without the right gear or training.

T13 works out the lashings for any load and direction; T12 plans the deck so restraint gear has room.

## Chapter 4: Access and Routes

A truck within the general mass and dimension limits can use most roads with no paperwork. Anything bigger or heavier runs only on roads approved for its class, either under a notice that covers everyone or a permit issued to you.

&#91;embedded content: Road access decision for a heavy vehicle, HVNL states, with WA and the NT\]

### The Words That Matter

| Term | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| General access | Vehicles within the general limits; no notice or permit needed |
| Restricted access vehicle | Anything over the general limits: B doubles, road trains, PBS combinations, oversize or overmass loads |
| Notice | A published approval for a class of vehicle on a set network, with conditions. Carry it, follow it |
| Permit | An approval issued to you, for your vehicle and route, after the road managers consent |
| Road manager | Whoever owns the road: a state agency for main roads, the local council for local roads |
| PBS (Performance Based Standards) | Vehicles approved on how they perform; each PBS vehicle has a network level it may use |
| HML (Higher Mass Limits) | Extra axle mass on approved routes, with conditions such as road friendly suspension |

### Plan the Route Before You Quote

1. Check the whole route on the NHVR National Network Map, from the depot gate to the customer's dock. The last few kilometres on a council road are where access problems often sit.
2. If any part needs a permit, apply early through the NHVR portal. A permit waits for every road manager on the route to consent.
3. Carry the notice or permit in the cab, and brief the driver on its conditions: speed, hours of travel, pilots or escorts, route.
4. Put access costs into the quote: a longer legal route, an escort, or a break down point all cost time (T10).

### Western Australia

Main Roads WA runs its own Restricted Access Vehicle networks, numbered by size: networks 1 to 4 for combinations up to 27.5 m, 5 to 8 up to 36.5 m, 9 and 10 up to 53.5 m, and longer PBS combinations by permit. RAVs over 20 m long, 2.5 m wide or 4.3 m high also need WA Heavy Vehicle Accreditation (Chapter 8). Check the Main Roads WA RAV mapping tool for each road.

## Chapter 5: Road Trains and the Outback

Road trains move freight across the north, the west and the inland on networks built for them. A Type 1 road train (an A double or B triple) runs to 36.5 m; a Type 2 (a triple) to 53.5 m. In NSW, Queensland, SA and Victoria they run under the National Class 2 Road Train Authorisation Notice 2026 (No. 2), in force from 1 August 2026 to 3 June 2030. WA and the NT run their own systems.

### Road Trains by State

| State | Types allowed | Longest | Speed cap | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| NSW | A double, B triple, AB triple, A triple, quads | 53.5 m | 90 km/h; PBS road trains 100 | Approved networks only, mostly the west |
| Victoria | A double only | 36.5 m | 100 km/h | No Type 2. B triples by permit only, on the north west network |
| Queensland | All types | 53.5 m | B triples 100 km/h; others 90 | Quad axle groups banned on state controlled roads |
| SA | All types | 53.5 m | 100 km/h on the Eyre Hwy west and Stuart Hwy north of Port Augusta; 90 elsewhere | 40 km/h in built up Orroroo, Peterborough, Port Augusta and Whyalla |
| WA | All types, by RAV network | 53.5 m; 60 m PBS by permit | 100 km/h for vehicles over 12 t; may be conditioned lower | WA Heavy Vehicle Accreditation needed |
| NT | All types | 53.5 m; longer by permit | 100 km/h if the vehicle meets NT standards regulation 32, otherwise 90 | Own rules, outside the HVNL |
| Tasmania | None |  |  | No road train network; B doubles only |
| ACT | None |  |  | No road train network |

The real speed limit is always the lowest of the road rules, the notice, the permit, road conditions and the posted sign ([NHVR road train speed limits](https://www.nhvr.gov.au/road-access/mass-dimension-and-loading/classes-of-heavy-vehicles/class-2/road-train-speed-limits)).

### Breaking Down at the Border

A road train that is legal in one state may not be in the next. A triple heading south from New South Wales must be broken down to an A double before it enters Victoria, because Victoria allows A doubles only. Find the assembly area before the trip, not at the border. T21 flags every leg where the combination is not allowed and shows where it must split.

### Planning an Outback Run

- **Fuel range:** know the distance between fuel stops on every leg, and carry a margin for detours and headwinds.
- **Heat and dust:** tyres, cooling systems and air filters work harder; check them at every stop.
- **Wet season closures:** in the north, roads can close for days or weeks between roughly November and April. Check road reports before departure and at every town.
- **Animals and cattle grids:** cattle, kangaroos and camels on unfenced roads; slow down for grids and stock crossings.
- **Breakdowns:** recovery can be hundreds of kilometres away. Carry water, food, spares and a satellite phone or personal locator beacon.
- **Live road conditions:** Live Traffic NSW, VicTraffic, QLDTraffic (13 19 40), SA Outback Road Warnings (1800 018 313), Main Roads WA Travel Map, and Road Report NT (1800 246 199).

T21 plans each leg: whether the combination is allowed, its planning speed, the driving time, and what to check before it leaves. Put the result through T14 to test the work and rest hours.

## Chapter 6: Chain of Responsibility for the Business

An operator sits at the centre of the chain: it is the employer, the scheduler, often the loader, and the business that sends the truck out. The law asks it to do everything reasonably practicable to make its transport activities safe, and asks its directors and managers to prove they checked.

### The Duties

| Section of the HVNL | Duty | In practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 26C | Primary duty: ensure, so far as reasonably practicable, the safety of your transport activities | Risks identified, controls in place, records kept |
| 26D | Executive officers must exercise due diligence | Directors and managers check the system works (below) |
| 26E | No asking, directing or requiring anyone to break speed, fatigue, mass, dimension or load restraint rules | Schedules and pay that allow legal trips |
| 26F | No contracts that encourage or reward those breaches | No bonus for impossible delivery times |

The top penalty for a Category 1 breach of the primary duty is $436,850 for an individual and $4,230,550 for a corporation (NHVR penalty list, checked November 2026).

&#91;embedded content: The six due diligence duties of an executive officer, HVNL section 26D, in plain words\]

### What a Regulator Looks For After an Incident

After a serious crash or a major breach, the question is what the business did before it happened. Investigators ask for:

- A written safety management system that matches what actually happens (Chapter 8, T25).
- A risk register with controls, owners and review dates.
- Driver records: licences, medicals, training, work diaries and records, fitness for duty checks.
- Schedules and booking records that show legal trip times.
- Maintenance and defect records (Chapter 11, T17).
- Contracts and rates with customers and subcontractors.
- Evidence that managers reviewed all of it and acted on problems.

If it is not written down, it did not happen. The forms listed in Chapter 15 are the paper trail.

## Chapter 7: Fatigue for the Business

The driver keeps the diary, but the business sets the roster. A fatigue regulated heavy vehicle is one over 12 t GVM or GCM, and every schedule that sends one out has to work inside the driver's legal hours, with queue time, loading time and traffic counted as work.

### Standard Hours or Alternative Compliance Hours

|  | Standard hours | Alternative Compliance Hours (ACH) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who can use it | Any operator, no accreditation | Accredited operators only |
| Hours | Fixed national tables for solo and two up drivers (Part C, Chapter 3) | Hours set in the accreditation, backed by the operator's own fatigue risk controls |
| What it replaced on 1 August 2026 | Unchanged | Basic and Advanced Fatigue Management (BFM and AFM); existing accreditations run to expiry |
| Best for | Most operators, most of the time | Operations whose work pattern does not fit the standard tables, with the systems to prove it is safe |

### Rostering That Stays Legal

1. **Plan every run against the rules before dispatch,** not after. T14 checks a planned trip against the counting rules: periods under 24 hours count from the end of any rest; 24 hours and longer count from the end of a major rest.
2. **Count all work, not just driving.** Loading, waiting at a dock, paperwork and refuelling are work time.
3. **Plan the 7 day and 14 day limits,** not just today: a 24 hour continuous rest in every 7 days, and in every 14 days four night rest breaks, two of them on consecutive days.
4. **Build in slack.** A schedule that only works on a clear road forces the driver to choose between being late and breaking the rules. That choice is your Chain of Responsibility risk (Chapter 6).
5. **Ask the driver.** Fitness for duty is now a legal duty: a driver who says they are too tired to start is following the law.

### Records the Business Keeps

- **Work diaries** for drivers working 100 km or more from base. Written diaries come from the state agency; electronic work diaries are a paid subscription, with an 8 minute tolerance on maximum work time.
- **Local area records** for work within 100 km, completed within 24 hours and kept by the record keeper for three years.
- **Rosters, run sheets and pay records** that line up with the diaries. An auditor compares them.

### Western Australia and the Northern Territory

WA and the NT sit outside the HVNL fatigue rules and manage fatigue under their own laws. An interstate fleet follows the rules of the state the truck is in, so a run from Adelaide to Perth changes rule set at the border. Plan the WA and NT legs against the local requirements and keep records that satisfy both.

## Chapter 8: Accreditation and Your Safety Management System

Accreditation is how an operator proves its systems work, in exchange for more flexibility. On 1 August 2026 the National Heavy Vehicle Accreditation Scheme (NHVAS) began its move to Heavy Vehicle Accreditation (HVA), with up to three years of transition.

### Heavy Vehicle Accreditation

| Part | What it covers | Why you would want it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| General Safety Accreditation | Your overall safety management, with maintenance, mass and fatigue modules | Many customers and head contractors ask for it; it shows due diligence |
| Alternative Compliance Accreditation | Approval to run Alternative Compliance Hours or other alternative arrangements | Hours or arrangements that fit your work, backed by your own risk controls |
| Safety Management System | Compulsory for anyone seeking accreditation | The backbone of the whole thing; worth having even if you never seek accreditation |

### WA Heavy Vehicle Accreditation

WA runs its own scheme, and it is mandatory, for WA and interstate operators alike, for any transport task connected with a business that:

- runs combinations over 50 t, or exceeds set axle group limits;
- runs restricted access vehicles over 20 m long, 2.5 m wide or 4.3 m high;
- holds period permits, or more than four single trip oversize or overmass permits a year; or
- uses the concessional loading scheme.

Vehicles of 8 t GVM or less, buses, special purpose vehicles and farm equipment are exempt ([Main Roads WA](https://www.mainroads.wa.gov.au/)).

### Building a Safety Management System

The NHVR structures a safety management system in five parts. It publishes guidance but no fill in templates, so T25 gives you the registers to start with:

| Part | What goes in it | Where T25 helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Leadership | Safety policy, who is responsible for what, how management stays involved | CoR duties register: each party, what it controls, and who in your business holds it |
| Risk | Hazards, risk ratings, controls and owners | Risk register with common transport risks filled in, scored and owned |
| People | Recruitment, licence and medical checks, training, fitness for duty | Training register with due dates and overdue flags |
| Assurance | Audits, incident investigation, reviews, corrective actions | Incident and near miss log that tracks open items |
| Safety systems | Procedures for fatigue, mass, loading, maintenance, speed, drugs and alcohol | The controls in the risk register point to T11, T13, T14 and T17; write your procedures around them |

Start small. A one truck operator needs a short policy, a risk register, a driver file and the daily forms. A fleet builds the same five parts out as it grows. Part E shows what to add at each stage.

## Chapter 9: Cost per km and Rate Setting

A busy truck can still lose money. If jobs are priced on what the market pays rather than on what the truck costs, every km can lose a little, and nobody notices until the bills land. Work out your cost per km first, then quote.

&#91;embedded content: T09 Truck cost per km, sample inputs, checked November 2026\]

### From Cost to Rate

On the T09 sample, the semi costs $2.75 per km, $137 per paid hour, or about $1,373 per working day. To make a 15% margin on the price, it needs to charge about $3.23 per km or $162 per hour.

```latex
\text{Rate} = \frac{\text{Cost}}{1 - \text{margin}}
```

The margin is on the price, not on the cost: adding 15% to cost gives only a 13% margin.

| Price by | Suits | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Per km | Linehaul and long runs | Empty return km must be priced in, or paid for by a backload |
| Per hour | Local, multi drop, waiting heavy work | Agree what time counts: gate to gate, or first drop to last |
| Per pallet or per tonne | Regular shared loads | Price must still cover the whole truck when it runs part full |
| Per trip | Repeat runs with a known route | Fuel levy and waiting time clauses, or the trip price erodes |

T10 turns your cost into a rate card by distance band, adds a fuel levy on the fuel share only, and checks the margin on each quote.

### Is Each Truck Earning Its Keep?

A truck that sits idle still costs its fixed share: on the sample, $1.35 of every km is fixed cost, spread over the km it actually runs. Fewer km means a higher cost per km. T18 tracks days used, loaded km and revenue against target for each truck, and T19 compares leasing, buying and hiring the next one.

## Chapter 10: Fuel, Fuel Tax Credits and the Road User Charge

Fuel is the biggest single cost of a heavy truck, and the fuel tax credit is the biggest single thing an operator can claim back. A heavy vehicle over 4.5 t GVM on a public road gets back the fuel excise less the road user charge: from 3 August 2026 that is 21.3 c/L, with the road user charge at 32.4 c/L ([ATO](https://www.ato.gov.au/businesses-and-organisations/income-deductions-and-concessions/incentives-and-concessions/fuel-schemes/fuel-tax-credits-business/rates-business/from-1-july-2026-to-30-june-2027)).

&#91;embedded content: ATO fuel tax credit rates for business, 1 July 2025 to November 2026\]

### What 2026 Did to the Rates

- **1 April to 30 June 2026:** excise was cut and the road user charge suspended. On road and off road credits both fell to 20.6 c/L.
- **1 July to 2 August 2026:** 20.2 c/L on road, 36.6 c/L for other business uses, with the road user charge at 16.4 c/L.
- **From 3 August 2026:** 21.3 c/L on road, 53.7 c/L for other uses, road user charge back at 32.4 c/L.

Claims follow the date the fuel was bought, not the date of the BAS. A BAS covering July and August 2026 splits across two rates. T15 holds every rate period in an editable table and works out the claim from your fuel records.

### Getting the Claim Right

1. Keep fuel records that show date, litres, vehicle and price; fuel cards make this easy.
2. Separate on road use from other uses (a refrigeration unit, a crane or PTO, idling on a private site). Other uses claim the higher rate.
3. Light vehicles (4.5 t GVM or less) on public roads cannot claim for that fuel.
4. Check the rates each February and August, when excise is indexed.

### Emissions Reporting

Large businesses now report emissions under AASB S2: Group 1 from 1 January 2025, Group 2 from 1 July 2026, Group 3 from 1 July 2027. Your customers may ask for your figures even if you are too small to report. Diesel produces 2.716 kg of CO2 equivalent per litre burned (Scope 1), plus 0.668 kg upstream (Scope 3), under the 2026 National Greenhouse Accounts factors. T22 turns your fuel and electricity use into Scope 1, 2 and 3 figures.

## Chapter 11: Maintenance and Fleet Systems

A truck that breaks down on the road costs the repair, the lost day, the recovery and sometimes the customer. A maintenance system is cheaper than any of those, and the HVNL expects one: keeping a vehicle roadworthy is part of the operator's primary duty.

### The Maintenance Loop

1. **Daily pre start** by the driver: tyres, lights, brakes, leaks, coupling, load restraint gear (T26 has the checklist).
2. **Defects reported in writing,** the same day, and assessed: fix now, fix before the next trip, or fix at the next service.
3. **Scheduled services** at the manufacturer's interval, by km or by date, whichever comes first. T17 tracks the next due date for every truck and trailer.
4. **Records kept** for every service, repair and defect, linked to the vehicle. An auditor or investigator will ask for them.

### Defect Notices

An officer who finds a defect at the roadside can issue a defect notice. A minor defect lets the vehicle keep running, with the repair due by a set date. A major defect means the vehicle cannot be used until the defect is fixed and cleared, apart from any movement the notice allows. Some notices can be cleared by the operator; others need an inspection. Check the notice for what applies.

Inspection rules differ by state: some require periodic inspections, others inspect on transfer or after a defect. Check your registering state's rules.

### Fleet Systems: What You Actually Need

| System | What it does | One truck | Small fleet | Larger fleet |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fuel card | Tracks every fill by truck and driver; easy fuel tax credit records | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Maintenance tracker | Service due dates, defects, records | A spreadsheet (T17) | T17 or simple software | Fleet maintenance software |
| Electronic work diary | Replaces the written diary, with the 8 minute tolerance | Optional | Worth it | Usually standard |
| Telematics | Location, speed, engine data, driver behaviour | Optional | Worth it | Standard |
| Dashcam | Evidence after an incident; driver coaching | Worth it | Yes | Yes |
| Transport management system (TMS) | Bookings, dispatch, PODs, invoicing | Not needed | Simple TMS | Full TMS |

Buy what solves a problem you have now. A one truck operator with a fuel card, T17, a dashcam and good paperwork is better covered than one with expensive software nobody uses.

## Chapter 12: Insurance

One uninsured crash can end a small transport business. The covers below are the core set; a broker who knows transport can tell you what your contracts and customers require on top.

| Cover | What it pays for | Who needs it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Compulsory third party (CTP) | Injury to people in a crash. Comes with registration in most states; bought separately as a green slip in NSW | Every registered vehicle, by law |
| Comprehensive motor | Damage to your truck and to other people's property | Anyone who cannot afford to replace the truck from cash |
| Third party property only | Damage to other people's property, not your truck | A cheaper floor for older trucks |
| Public liability | Injury or damage you cause away from the road: at a customer's dock, in their yard | Almost every operator; most customers ask for a certificate |
| Goods in transit (carrier's liability) | Loss or damage to customers' freight while you carry it | Anyone carrying other people's goods |
| Marine transit | Freight on a sea or international leg | Operators who arrange sea legs, such as Bass Strait |
| Workers compensation | Injury to employees at work | Every employer, by law, in every state |
| Income protection | Your income if you cannot drive | Owner drivers, who have no workers comp cover of their own |

### Read the Exclusions

Reasons a transport claim can be refused include an unlicensed or unlisted driver, an overloaded or unroadworthy vehicle, an undeclared dangerous goods load, poor load restraint, or freight not covered by the policy's list of goods. Every one of those is also a compliance failure. Good compliance keeps the insurance working.

## Chapter 13: Tolls and Urban Rules

City work is short distances, slow traffic, and tolls that add up quickly. In Sydney, a heavy vehicle's toll is roughly three times the car toll on most motorways, and a few runs a day add up fast.

### Heavy Vehicle Toll Classes

| City | Heavy vehicle class | Who is in it | Pricing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sydney | Class B | Over 2.8 m high or over 12.5 m long | Same price day and night; changes quarterly (WestConnex each 1 January) |
| Melbourne, CityLink and West Gate Tunnel | HCV; Long HCV | Rigid trucks with 3 or more axles or over 4.5 t, buses, articulated trucks; Long HCV is 26 m or longer | Day (6 am to 8 pm) and night prices; changes quarterly |
| Melbourne, EastLink | HCV | Anything over 4.5 t GVM, plus buses over 12 seats | Same price day, night and weekend; changes each 1 July |
| Brisbane | Class 4 | Heavy commercial vehicles | Peak and off peak on Clem7, Legacy Way and Go Between Bridge (peak 5 am to 8 pm); changes each 1 July |

### Trip Caps

Long trips on one network are capped: CityLink $38.32 by day and $25.55 at night; CityLink plus West Gate Tunnel $38.38; WestConnex full length $38.22; EastLink $21.33; Westlink M7 $31.71 (account prices including GST, checked November 2026). T16 applies each cap once per trip.

### Rules That Catch Trucks in Cities

- **NorthConnex:** trucks over 12.5 m long or 2.8 m high travelling between the M1 and M2 in Sydney must use NorthConnex, unless their pickup or delivery can only be reached from Pennant Hills Road. Fine: $215. Placarded dangerous goods loads must use Pennant Hills Road instead.
- **Tunnels and dangerous goods:** placarded dangerous goods are banned from the West Gate Tunnel in Melbourne, and many other urban tunnels restrict them. Check every tunnel on the route.
- **Curfews:** many councils restrict truck movements at night in residential streets, and some sites set their own delivery hours. Build them into the schedule.
- **Loading zones:** time limits and vehicle types are signed zone by zone, and fines are common. Book a dock or a slot where you can.
- **Clearways and bus lanes:** peak hour clearways and bus lanes apply to trucks too.

Add the yearly toll bill to T09 so it is part of your cost per km, and pass tolls through on quotes in T10, either built in or itemised.

## Chapter 14: Special Freight

Some freight brings its own rules on top of everything above. Each type below needs extra licences, gear, training or paperwork, and each is priced higher for a reason.

| Freight | Extra rules | What the operator needs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dangerous goods | The Australian Dangerous Goods Code, applied through each state's DG laws. Placard loads need a licensed driver and a licensed vehicle | DG driver and vehicle licences from the state agency, transport documents, emergency information, segregation, placards, and route checks for tunnel bans |
| Refrigerated | Food safety rules and the customer's temperature specification | Pre cooled units, temperature logs for every trip, a plan for a unit failure. Fuel for the fridge unit can claim the higher fuel tax credit rate |
| Oversize or overmass | Class 1 notices or permits, travel time restrictions, pilots or escorts | Route surveys, permit conditions in the cab, signs and lights, escort bookings |
| Livestock | Land transport animal welfare standards; loading density and time off water rules | Trained drivers, stock crates in good order, records of loading and spelling times |
| Bulk and tippers | Mass is the main risk: loads are easy to overfill | Weighing at the loading site or on board scales; HML where the route and vehicle qualify |
| Car carriers and livestock crates | Some may run higher than 4.3 m on approved routes | The notice or permit for the height, and route checks for bridges and wires |

### Dangerous Goods by State

Every state and territory applies the Australian Dangerous Goods Code through its own law and its own agency. The licences are not always interchangeable for every purpose, so check the agency in each state you run through.

- Before a DG load: confirm the classification on the transport documents, check segregation with other freight, and check the route for tunnel and bridge bans.
- In the cab: transport documents, emergency information and the right safety equipment for the class carried.
- Placarded loads in Sydney must use Pennant Hills Road, not NorthConnex, and are banned from the West Gate Tunnel in Melbourne (Chapter 13).

The DG checklist in the operator forms pack (Chapter 15) covers loading, documents, placards and route bans. This chapter is an overview, not DG training: anyone loading or driving DG needs the accredited course for their role.
