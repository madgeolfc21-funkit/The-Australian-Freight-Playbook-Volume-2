## Chapter 1: Choosing the Mode

Pick the cheapest mode that still arrives in time, then check it is reliable enough for this freight. Every mode gets the same three tests: does it meet the deadline door to door, what does it cost on chargeable weight, and how much risk does it add.

### When Each Mode Wins

| Mode | Wins when | Loses when | Cubic factor T05 uses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Road | Most general freight, door to door, under about 2,000 km, or when time matters | Very long distances in volume, where rail is cheaper | 250 kg per cubic metre |
| Rail | Long distances in volume, especially east to west; dense freight; when a day or two longer is fine | Short lanes, small consignments, tight deadlines; the east coast, where road dominates | As your quote says (the sample uses 333) |
| Air | Urgent, small, high value; remote communities without good road access | Heavy or bulky freight; dangerous goods that air will not take | 167 kg per cubic metre |
| Sea | Tasmania; bulky, heavy, low value freight with time to spare | Anything urgent | 1,000 kg per cubic metre |

Rail and sea run to timetables, so add pickup, terminal cut off and delivery days to their quoted transit. Compare modes door to door, never terminal to terminal.

### A Worked Example From T05

An 850 kg consignment of 3.2 cubic metres must arrive within 4 days.

| Mode | Quote ex GST, all in | Door to door days | Meets 4 days? | Total score |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Road | $1,180 | 3 | Yes | 90 |
| Rail | $820 | 5 | No | Knocked out |
| Air | $4,300 | 1 | Yes | 53 |
| Sea | $690 | 9 | No | Knocked out |

Rail and sea are cheaper but miss the deadline, so they are out. Of the two left, road is far cheaper and reliable enough, so it wins. Give the same freight 6 days and rail becomes the answer: always ask how much time the receiver really needs.

T05 weighs cost against service at 60:40 by default; change the weighting to suit the freight.

## Chapter 2: Rail and Intermodal

Rail carries most of Australia's bulk freight and most non bulk freight on the long haul to Perth. On the east coast it carries only a small share, which is why road owns the Melbourne to Sydney lane. Nationally, only 16% of non bulk freight moved by rail in 2024 to 25 ([Infrastructure Australia](https://www.infrastructureaustralia.gov.au/ipl/high-productivity-freight-networks/rail-freight)).

### When Rail Beats Road

- **Distance:** the longer the haul, the more rail's lower cost per tonne km outweighs the extra handling at each end.
- **Density:** heavy freight in volume suits rail; light, bulky freight fills a container before it earns its keep.
- **Time to spare:** trains run to timetables, and the terminal steps add time. If the receiver can wait a day or two, rail is worth a quote.

### Corridors and Operators

| Corridor | Rail's role | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| East coast to Perth | Rail carries most non bulk freight | The main case for general freight by rail |
| Adelaide to Darwin | Rail and road both run | Via Alice Springs |
| Melbourne to Sydney, Sydney to Brisbane | Small share; road dominates | Inland Rail is meant to change Melbourne to Brisbane over time |

Interstate intermodal operators include Pacific National, Aurizon, SCT Logistics and Qube. Each runs its own terminals, timetables and booking rules, so get quotes from more than one.

### Inland Rail

In May 2026 the federal government stopped Inland Rail at Parkes. Beveridge (north of Melbourne) to Parkes is due by the end of 2027, and the corridor north of Parkes is only being preserved for later ([Inland Rail](https://inlandrail.com.au/progress/)). For Melbourne to Brisbane freight, plan on road for the life of this edition.

## Chapter 3: Air Freight

Air is the fastest mode and by far the dearest per kilogram. It earns its place for urgent, small, high value freight, and for remote communities that road cannot reach reliably.

### Volumetric Weight Rules Everything

Air charges on the greater of actual weight and volumetric weight, commonly at 167 kg per cubic metre (6,000 cubic centimetres per kilogram). A 60 × 40 × 40 cm carton weighing 8 kg is charged as 16 kg by air. Packaging that would cost nothing extra on a truck can double an air bill: pack tight, and use T01 to compare.

### Before You Book

| Check | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Cut off time at the airline or forwarder's terminal | Miss it and the freight waits for the next flight; check whether it is the same day or the night before |
| Screening | Air cargo is security screened, or comes from a business approved under the government's Known Consignor scheme. Unscreened freight can be delayed or refused; ask your carrier what applies to your service |
| Dangerous goods | Air rules are much tighter than road. Many items that go by truck are restricted or forbidden by air |
| Size and weight per piece | Smaller aircraft on regional routes take smaller and lighter pieces |
| Delivery at the other end | Collect from the airport, or book a road delivery leg |

### Lithium Batteries and Other Dangerous Goods

Lithium batteries are the most common dangerous goods problem in air freight. Batteries shipped on their own, without equipment, face the strictest limits, and some can only go on cargo aircraft. Batteries packed with or inside equipment are allowed in more cases, with labelling and quantity limits. Aerosols, paints, adhesives, fuels, gases and some cleaning products are also restricted. Declare every one: undeclared dangerous goods by air carry serious penalties, and the airline's dangerous goods desk is the place to check before you book.

### Freight to Remote Communities

Many remote communities rely on air for urgent freight, and on road or barge for everything else. Weight and size limits on small aircraft are tight, prices are high, and weather and wet season closures can strand freight. Plan regular supplies by road or sea well ahead, and keep air for what cannot wait.

## Chapter 4: Sea and Bass Strait

Every interstate move to or from Tasmania includes a sea leg. For the rest of the country, sea means containers through the ports and a small amount of coastal shipping.

### Bass Strait

Freight crosses on the Spirit of Tasmania ferries and on dedicated freight ships to the northern Tasmanian ports. The Spirit of Tasmania now sails from Geelong to Devonport; Spirit of Tasmania IV and V began sailing on 31 October 2026.

- **Plan around the sailing,** not the road. A missed cut off can mean a day's delay, and peak periods such as school holidays book out.
- **Price the whole chain:** road to the port, the crossing, and road from the port. Ask whether the quote includes wharf and handling charges.
- **Check the Tasmanian Freight Equalisation Scheme.** It provides freight assistance to eligible businesses that ship eligible goods by sea across Bass Strait, northbound and southbound, and to King Island and the Furneaux Group ([Services Australia](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/tasmanian-freight-equalisation-scheme)). Assistance rose by 25% from July 2025, and an independent review of the scheme was due to report to the Australian Government by mid 2026, so check the current rules before you rely on it.

### Containers in Brief

| Item | What to know |
| --- | --- |
| Sizes | 20 foot and 40 foot are standard; high cube 40 foot gives extra height |
| Australian pallets | The square 1165 mm pallet does not fit a container neatly: about 10 in a 20 foot and 20 in a 40 foot, single stacked |
| Weight | Road limits at each end can be lower than the container's own limit; check the truck can legally carry it (Part D) |
| Packing | The packer declares the verified gross mass for export containers; restraint inside the container matters as much as on a truck |

### Detention and Demurrage

Shipping lines and terminals give a set number of free days. After that:

- **Demurrage** is charged while a full container stays at the terminal past its free days.
- **Detention** is charged while you keep the container outside the terminal past its free days, until the empty is returned.

Both run per container per day and climb fast. Book the delivery truck, the unload and the empty return before the ship arrives. Part B, Chapter 12 shows how free days work on an import, and Vol. 1, Warehouse Operations has the full guide.

## Chapter 5: Last Mile and Ecommerce

The last mile is the most expensive part of a parcel's journey per kilometre, and the part your customer sees. Choose the service by where the parcels go and what the customer expects, not only by price.

### The Options

| Option | Suits | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Australia Post and StarTrack | Coverage everywhere, including rural and remote addresses; parcel lockers and post offices for collection | Size and weight limits on standard parcel services |
| Courier networks | Metro and regional business deliveries, larger parcels | Remote postcodes handed to another carrier; remote area surcharges |
| Aggregators | One account and one label system across several carriers; good for small senders | The aggregator is not the carrier: claims still depend on who moved it (Part B, Chapter 2) |
| Same day and on demand | Urgent metro deliveries | Price per drop; coverage only in the main cities |
| Your own vans | Dense local delivery where you control the customer experience | The second rule book for light vehicles (Part E, Chapter 6) |

### Failed Deliveries and Returns

A failed delivery costs a second trip, a redelivery fee or a trip to a collection point, and often a customer complaint. Many failures can be prevented at checkout:

- Capture a phone number and delivery instructions with every order.
- Offer parcel lockers, post offices or a safe place option for customers who are out during the day.
- Validate addresses at checkout, and flag rural and remote postcodes so the delivery promise matches reality.
- Send tracking notifications, so the customer knows when to expect it.

Returns are a freight cost too. Price them in T07 alongside outbound freight, and decide in advance who pays.

### Rural and Remote

Delivery to rural and remote addresses takes longer and costs more, and some carriers do not go there at all. Set honest delivery times by postcode, and test the slowest postcodes you serve with a real parcel before you promise anything on your website.

## Chapter 6: State Notes

| Where | What makes it different | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tasmania | A sea leg on every interstate move; sailing schedules and cut offs set the transit time | Book around the sailings; check the Tasmanian Freight Equalisation Scheme (Chapter 4) |
| Northern Territory | Long distances; road trains on the main routes; Adelaide to Darwin by road or rail; wet season closures in the north | Ask for road and rail quotes to Darwin; plan stock ahead of the wet season |
| Northern WA | Very long road distances from Perth; wet season closures in the Kimberley and Pilbara | Build buffer time into delivery promises from about November to April |
| ACT | No port; general freight arrives by road, mostly from Sydney | Price Canberra as a Sydney lane plus about 290 km |
| East coast | Road dominates; rail carries very little general freight between Melbourne, Sydney and Brisbane | Default to road; quote rail only for long, heavy, patient freight |
| East to west | Rail carries most non bulk freight to Perth | Get rail quotes for anything that can wait a few days |
